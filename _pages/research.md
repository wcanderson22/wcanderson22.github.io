## Research Overview

My research focuses on understanding the evolutionary processes that generate and maintain biodiversity, with particular emphasis on evolutionary genomics, speciation, and the genomic architecture of divergence. I am especially interested in how structural genomic variation, hybridization, and geographic isolation influence evolutionary trajectories and contribute to the formation and maintenance of species boundaries.

To investigate these questions, I integrate whole-genome sequencing, population and comparative genomics, phylogenetics, bioinformatics, and field-based natural history. Much of my research focuses on birds, which provide compelling opportunities to investigate the genomic mechanisms underlying evolutionary diversification.

My current research program encompasses three interconnected areas:

---

### [Sex Chromosome Evolution in Birds](/research/sex-chromosomes/)

Sex chromosomes are among the most dynamic components of vertebrate genomes, frequently undergoing structural rearrangements, changes in recombination, and patterns of differentiation distinct from those of autosomes. Understanding how these chromosomes evolve can provide insight into the genomic mechanisms underlying adaptation, divergence, and speciation.

My research investigates sex chromosome evolution in birds, with particular emphasis on chromosomal fusions, inversions, recombination suppression, and the formation of neo-sex chromosomes. Using comparative genomic approaches, I examine how these structural changes arise and vary across closely related species and populations.

Current projects investigate neo-sex chromosome evolution in North American chickadees (*Poecile*) and chromosomal rearrangements in Northern House Wrens (*Troglodytes aedon*).

---

### [Genomic Divergence and Hybridization](/research/hybridization/)

Hybrid zones provide natural opportunities to investigate the evolutionary forces that maintain or erode species boundaries. By examining patterns of genomic differentiation and introgression, we can better understand how reproductive isolation evolves and how populations remain distinct despite ongoing gene flow.

My research explores the genomic architecture of divergence, focusing on how structural variants, recombination landscapes, and heterogeneous genomic differentiation influence evolutionary outcomes.

I investigate these processes in several avian systems, including hybridization between eastern and western Northern House Wrens and between Black-capped and Carolina Chickadees. These systems provide opportunities to examine how different genomic regions contribute to divergence and how evolutionary processes vary across geographic contexts.

[**Explore Genomic Divergence and Hybridization Research →**](/research/hybridization/)

---

### [Avian Phylogeography and Speciation](/research/phylogeography/)

Understanding how biodiversity originates requires examining the evolutionary histories of populations and species across geographic and ecological landscapes. Phylogeographic and phylogenomic approaches allow us to reconstruct these histories, identify cryptic evolutionary diversity, and investigate the processes that contribute to lineage divergence.

My research integrates genomic, morphological, and behavioral data to investigate evolutionary relationships, population structure, and species boundaries. I am particularly interested in cases where substantial genomic differentiation occurs despite limited phenotypic divergence, as well as systems in which traditional taxonomic classifications may not accurately reflect evolutionary history.

Current projects include investigating evolutionary differentiation within the Northern Pygmy-Owl (*Glaucidium gnoma*) complex and comparative phylogenomic relationships across chickadees and their relatives (Paridae).
